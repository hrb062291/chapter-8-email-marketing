Email Marketing

Social media and ad networks rent you attention. Your email list is the one audience you own, and no algorithm decides whether your message reaches it. This page covers how to build a list, stay out of the spam folder, write emails people open, and measure and test what works.

Why email still matters

Email feels old. It's still one of the most profitable things a marketer can do.

Volume
347B

Emails sent and received every day worldwide (2023), on track for about 392 billion by 2026.

Preference
#1

The channel U.S. millennials most prefer for hearing from brands, well ahead of text and social media (2022 survey).

Return
Top ROI

Marketers consistently rate email as their highest-return channel.

Why is the return so high? Once someone gives you their address, each extra email costs almost nothing. With display or search ads, you pay again for every impression or click. Plenty of emails never get opened, but a small share of a huge number is still a lot of people. (One store email to 10 million customers counts as 10 million of those daily emails.)

Rent attention everywhere else; own it here. That's why companies guard their email lists so closely.

Spam vs. email marketing

The difference isn't the email. It's whether the person asked for it.

Black hat

Spam

Scrape addresses from websites or buy a list, then blast it. It mostly works for impulse products, because nobody rationally buys a lawn mower from a stranger's email. It's illegal in many countries and it wrecks your ability to reach anyone.

White hat

Permission marketing

The customer gives you their address and permission. You send what they signed up for: deals, useful information or entertaining content.

Because losing a real email is worse, filters lean toward letting some spam through. A marketer's job is to look as little like spam as possible, because the filter isn't sure either.

Who decides if your email gets delivered

Mailbox providers

Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo and Apple watch how people treat your email. Do they open it, read it, reply, or hit "Report spam"? Poor engagement pushes all your mail toward junk.

Spam traps

A pristine trap is an address no person ever used, so mail to it proves you scraped or bought a list. A non-pristine trap is an old abandoned address, so mail to it proves you never clean your list.

Content filters

Warning signs: ALL CAPS, piles of exclamation marks, spammy words, an email that's mostly one big image, and sloppy HTML.

Updated rules (2024): Gmail and Yahoo now require anyone sending about 5,000+ emails a day to prove who they are with email authentication (SPF, DKIM and DMARC), offer a one-click unsubscribe and honor it within two days, and keep spam complaints below 0.3%. Microsoft added similar rules for Outlook in 2025.
The law: in the U.S., CAN-SPAM requires a working unsubscribe link, your physical mailing address and honest subject lines. The EU (GDPR) and Canada (CASL) go further and require permission before you send.

Practice: you're the spam filter

Inbox or spam folder? Decide for each email.

Today, getting delivered depends more on who you are and how people react to you than on the words in your email.

Building and cleaning your list

Your list is the asset. Build it with permission and keep it clean.

Rule 1

Never buy lists

Bought lists are full of spam traps and people who never agreed to hear from you.

Rule 2

Collect everywhere

Checkout, account sign-up, Wi-Fi login, receipts, contests, warranty registration, loyalty programs.

Rule 3

Get permission

And tell people what's in it for them. "Sign up for our newsletter" isn't a reason.

List-cleaning checklist

1
Hard bounce (the address doesn't exist): remove it immediately. Most email platforms do this for you.
2
Soft bounce (full inbox, server down): remove after about five in a row.
3
Non-openers: remove anyone inactive for a long time, a year at most. Send one "Still want these?" email first.
4
Double opt-in (confirm by clicking a link) keeps the list cleaner than single opt-in and blocks typos and fake sign-ups.
5
Unsubscribe link in every marketing email. Honor it right away.
A smaller list of people who open beats a bigger list of people who don't. Non-openers drag down your reputation with every mailbox provider.

Types of email

Different emails have different jobs and different rules.

Broadcast

Newsletter / promotion

Sent to the whole list or a segment: sales, new products, content.

Broadcast

Onboarding

A short series for brand-new subscribers that sets up the relationship.

Broadcast

Triggered

Sent to one person because of something they did, like abandoning a cart. Only goes to people on the list.

Not marketing

Transactional

Receipts, shipping notices, password resets. The customer needs these.

Rules for transactional emails

1
No marketing permission needed, because the customer needs the information.
2
The subject line says what it is: "Your order has shipped."
3
Transaction details go at the top.
4
Any promotion comes after them and takes up less than half the email.
Transactional emails get opened more than almost anything else, so a little promotion inside them is valuable. Too much turns a receipt into spam.

Email metrics

Five numbers tell you whether an email worked.

Open rate = Opened ÷ Delivered
Click-through rate = Clicked ÷ Delivered
Conversion rate = Conversions ÷ Clicks
Average order value = Revenue ÷ Conversions
Churn rate = Unsubscribes ÷ Addresses on list
Revenue per email = Revenue ÷ Delivered
Watch the denominator: click-through rate divides by emails delivered, not opened. Clicks ÷ opens is a different metric called click-to-open rate.
Updated (2021): Apple Mail's privacy feature loads emails in the background, which counts them as "opened" even if nobody reads them. Open rates now look higher than they really are, so most marketers use click-through rate as their main success metric.

Cases from the chapter

One offer or a dozen? Two retailers compared
A fashion retailer sent an email with one offer (a men's sale) and nothing else. A home-improvement retailer led with one offer (custom garage doors) and then stacked a dozen more below it. Which works better depends on how many readers care about the main offer. Lots of fashion shoppers might want a men's sale. Very few home-improvement shoppers are buying a garage door this week, so the extra offers ("Ooh, fence installation!") give everyone else something to click.
The plain-text B2B sales email
No images at all, just text. It can still work: plain text looks like a note from a person, and business buyers are reading for information. It also asked readers to add the sender to their safe-sender list and had an unsubscribe link at the bottom.
The news-satire newsletter
For a media site, an email list is an easy win because the content already exists. The email looked like the website, with several headlines at the top. The site's traffic data showed which stories were most popular, so those went first. Every click is a page view, and page views earn ad revenue.
Two unsubscribe pages that got it wrong
One asked why you were leaving (good, it collects data) but had no option to get fewer emails instead of none. The other made you uncheck seven boxes, with no "unsubscribe from everything." People who can't leave easily click "Report spam" instead, which hurts the sender far more than an unsubscribe.

Triggered and onboarding emails

The best emails respond to something a person just did.

Trigger

Abandoned cart

Remind them of the product. Maybe offer a coupon.

Trigger

Purchase

Send the receipt, then suggest products that go with it.

Trigger

Past purchase

Ask for a review or feedback on the experience.

Abandoned-cart emails are among the most profitable emails a company sends, because the person almost bought. Sending within a few hours usually beats waiting until the next day. In a 2022 survey of U.S. shoppers, current promotions were the emails most likely to be opened (62%), followed by shipping and order confirmations (52% each): people open emails about something they already did.

Think about it: should an abandoned-cart email include a coupon? It raises sales, but customers learn to leave items in their cart on purpose to get one. How would you get the benefit without teaching that habit?

Onboarding: the first few emails

A new subscriber knows less about you, is more curious and is easier to influence than someone who's been on your list for a year. The first emails set the habit, whether that's opening or ignoring. So new sign-ups get a short series of their own (usually 3–5 emails) before they join the regular list.

Email typeIts job

Timing matters: send the welcome right away, then space the rest out (for example 1, 2, 5 and 10 days later). Build in dependencies: someone who clicks everything gets a different path than someone who opens nothing, and nobody who just upgraded should get an exit survey.

Case: the file-storage app's one-line reminder
Sent to people who signed up but never installed the app. It said why they were getting the email, gave two benefits and put the download button right there. One email, one action.
Case: the home-decor store's "We miss you"
A 20%-off, limited-time offer in a friendly handwritten style, sent only to people who had ignored the earlier onboarding emails. The discount went only where a sale was unlikely anyway, instead of giving away margin to everyone.

A/B testing: how many people should you test on?

Every email platform can test two versions of a subject line or email. The hard part is deciding how big the test should be.

The setup

15% get A, 15% get B

From a list of about 59,400, version A goes to 8,910 people and version B to another 8,910. After 24 hours, the winner goes to the remaining 70% (41,584 people).

The result

A: 12.9% vs. B: 12.3%

A wins by 0.6 points. Sending A to the other 41,584 people means 41,584 × 0.6% = about 250 extra people see the email.

The catch: was A really better?

A 0.6-point gap with about 8,900 people per version is not statistically significant (p = 0.23). A might have won by luck. Two forces pull against each other:

↑
Bigger test: you're more confident in the result, but more people get the worse version.
↓
Smaller test: more people get the winner, but you're more likely to pick the wrong one.
Traditional

Size for 80% power

Pick a size with an 80% chance of detecting a real difference. To detect a 1-point difference at a 15% open rate, you need about 20,000 people per version. Most companies don't have 40,000 subscribers to spare.

Feit & Berman

Size for profit

Pick the size that gets the best results across the whole list. Then send whichever version did better, even if the difference isn't significant. If two versions are too close to tell apart, picking the "wrong" one costs almost nothing.

Traditional: n ≈ 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² × p(1 − p) ÷ δ²
Profit-maximizing: n = √( N/4 × (s/σ)² + (¾ × (s/σ)²)² ) − ¾ × (s/σ)²

The questions

Work these in the spreadsheet. Open a hint only if you're stuck.

1. Which respondents should be removed from the email list?
Hint: go back to the list-cleaning checklist. Which behaviors in the data match it?
2. What is the best time of day to send emails?
Hint: compare click and conversion rates by time, not just opens. Open rates are inflated.
3. Which offers generate the best response?
Hint: "best" could mean clicks, conversions or revenue. A buy-one-get-one offer can win clicks and still lose money. Decide which measure matters and say why.
4. Should Snappos split the list by gender and send each group only its own shoes?
Hint: check the data before assuming. Does anyone click on or buy the other gender's shoes? Think about gifts.
5. Snappos will soon run a sale on women's high heels. Who should get that email?
Hint: segment by what people actually clicked and bought, not just gender. Which related categories signal interest?
